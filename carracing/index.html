CarRacing RL: Generative Trajectory Aggregation (GTA)

Course project for 10-703 Deep Reinforcement Learning exploring how diffusion models, distillation, and DAgger can be combined to improve continuous control in the CarRacing-v2 environment.

Introduction

Standard imitation learning in continuous-control tasks is heavily constrained by the quantity and quality of expert data. Once a small set of demonstrations has been collected, it is difficult to cover the wide variety of states that arise when a learned policy starts to drift off the expert’s trajectory.

This project, called GTA: Generative Trajectory Aggregation, investigates whether a trajectory diffusion model can act as a generative expert for CarRacing-v2. The central idea is to train a diffusion policy on expert rollouts, use it to generate synthetic expert trajectories, and then distill these trajectories into a lightweight student policy via behavior cloning and DAgger. The goal is to improve sample efficiency and robustness compared to training directly on the limited original expert data.

Methods

Environment and Expert Data

All experiments are conducted in the Gymnasium CarRacing-v2 environment with grayscale observations resized to 84×84 and stacked over four frames. The expert policy is an off-the-shelf continuous-action PPO controller trained on CarRacing-v2; its rollouts provide the initial demonstration dataset of state–action trajectories.

Trajectory Diffusion Expert

The diffusion expert is a policy diffusion transformer that predicts the noise added to future actions conditioned on a history of states and actions. Training follows the standard denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) setup:

  • Expert trajectories are padded and masked to handle variable lengths.
  • States and actions are normalized using global statistics over valid time-steps.
  • For each batch element, a diffusion timestep t and Gaussian noise are sampled.
  • Noisy actions are formed and the transformer predicts the noise to be removed.
  • Loss is mean-squared error on non-padded entries.

After training, the model generates trajectories by running the reverse diffusion process: starting from Gaussian noise in action space and iteratively denoising using the learned transformer and an inference scheduler.

Student Policies and Distillation

To evaluate the benefit of synthetic trajectories, two student policies are compared:

  • A baseline student trained only on the original PPO expert demonstrations.
  • A distilled student trained on a larger dataset of diffusion-generated synthetic trajectories.

The student is a compact convolutional policy: a CNN encoder maps stacked frames to a state embedding, followed by fully connected layers that output steering, gas, and brake. Training uses standard behavior cloning with mean-squared error on continuous actions.

DAgger Pipeline

On top of offline distillation, a lightweight DAgger framework is used to collect on-policy data. The implementation is designed to be expert-agnostic: any expert (PPO or diffusion) only needs to implement an act(obs) method.

  • The student runs in the environment and visits states that may lie off the original expert trajectories.
  • At each visited state, the expert provides a corrective action label.
  • State–action pairs are added to an ImitationDataset that grows over DAgger iterations.
  • The student is periodically updated on the aggregated dataset.

Results

As a first validation step, the diffusion pipeline was tested on synthetic trajectory data with sinusoidal patterns in the actions. The diffusion expert’s noise prediction loss decreased from about 1.30 at the beginning of training to around 0.08–0.18 in later stages, confirming that the model can learn to denoise structured action trajectories instead of memorizing noise.

The DAgger loop was then run with a PPO expert on real CarRacing-v2 rollouts. A student trained on 5,000 expert samples achieved higher returns than the raw expert dataset (average return ≈ −23.6 vs. ≈ −45). Over five DAgger iterations, the dataset expanded to 20,000 samples. Imitation loss decreased steadily, while evaluation returns fluctuated between roughly −49 and −18, reflecting the instability of PPO’s corrective labels but also demonstrating that the pipeline is functioning end-to-end.

These preliminary results show that:

  • The diffusion expert can model trajectory-level structure on toy data.
  • The DAgger infrastructure successfully aggregates off-distribution states.
  • Even with imperfect experts, student policies can outperform the initial demos.

Discussion

GTA highlights both the promise and the practical challenges of using diffusion models as generative experts in interactive control settings. On the positive side, trajectory diffusion offers a principled way to synthesize new expert-like rollouts that preserve global structure and can be used for distillation. However, integrating diffusion with DAgger requires careful data matching, consistent preprocessing, and stable expert labels.

The next stage of this work focuses on training the diffusion expert directly on PPO trajectories from CarRacing-v2, generating large synthetic datasets, and comparing three conditions:

  • Student trained only on original expert data.
  • Student trained on diffusion-generated trajectories.
  • Student trained with DAgger where the diffusion model serves as the expert and provides generative recovery behavior.

Longer-term, the project aims to replace PPO trajectories with real F1 telemetry, converting racing logs into observation–action sequences compatible with the current pipeline. This would test whether diffusion-based supervision can generalize from simulation to structured real-world driving data.
